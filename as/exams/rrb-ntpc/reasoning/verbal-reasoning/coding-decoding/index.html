    সংকেতীকৰণ আৰু অৱসংকেতীকৰণৰ ফান্দবোৰ আগতীয়াকৈ অনুমানযোগ্য। আটাইতকৈ সাধাৰণটো হ'ল ভুল দিশত সৰকণ প্ৰয়োগ কৰা, বিশেষকৈ সংকেতটো Z ৰ ফালে পিছলৈ গ'লে। দ্বিতীয়টো হ'ল এনে সৰকণ যি শব্দৰ মাজতে সলনি হয়, যাক প্ৰতিটো আখৰত নিয়ম পৰীক্ষা কৰি ধৰিব লাগে, কেৱল প্ৰথমটোত নহয়। তৃতীয়টো হ'ল ভুল ক্ৰমত প্ৰয়োগ কৰা এটা সংযুক্ত নিয়ম। চতুৰ্থটো হ'ল এনে এটা প্ৰতিস্থাপন প্ৰশ্ন য'ত উদাহৰণে আচলতে দুটা বা তিনিৰ সৰকণ দেখুওৱাৰ সময়ত পৰীক্ষাৰ্থীয়ে একৰ সৰকণ ধৰি লয়। পঞ্চমটো হ'ল এটা স্থান প্ৰশ্ন য'ত পৰীক্ষাৰ্থীয়ে এটা আখৰ একেৰে ভুল গণে। গোটেই শব্দটো তাৰ সংকেতৰ লগত আখৰে আখৰে তুলনা কৰি যোৰবোৰ লিখিলে এই ভুলবোৰৰ বেছিভাগ আঁতৰি যায়।

    সংকেতীকৰণ-অৱসংকেতীকৰণ আন কেইবাটাও যুক্তিতৰ্ক বিষয়ৰ সৈতে ওচৰ সম্পৰ্কত আছে। ই এটা শব্দত প্ৰয়োগ কৰা আখৰ শৃংখলাই, গতিকে আখৰ শৃংখলাত সাৱলীলতাই ইয়াক প্ৰায় প্ৰচেষ্টাহীন কৰি তোলে। আখৰ পৰীক্ষা আৰু শব্দ গঠনে একেই স্থান তালিকা ব্যৱহাৰ কৰে। সাদৃশ্যই দুটা বস্তুৰ মাজৰ সম্পৰ্ক সোধে, যি এটা নিয়মৰ নাম দি প্ৰয়োগ কৰাৰ একেই দক্ষতা। আৰু অ-মৌখিক অংশৰ দাপোণ-প্ৰতিবিম্ব প্ৰশ্নই একেই বিপৰীত আখৰৰ যোৰ ব্যৱহাৰ কৰে। ইয়াত স্বচ্ছন্দ হোৱা পৰীক্ষাৰ্থীয়ে একেসময়তে তিনি বা চাৰিটা আন বিষয়ত আগবাঢ়ে।

    অভ্যাসত লগা সংকেতৰ নিয়মবোৰৰ বাবে টোকাবহীত এখন পৃষ্ঠা ৰখা ভাল। ইয়াক সৰকণৰ নিয়ম, স্থানৰ নিয়ম, বিন্যাসৰ নিয়ম আৰু বিপৰীত নিয়মত ভাগ কৰি, নতুন উদাহৰণ পোৱাৰ লগে লগে তাৰ শিৰোনামাৰ তলত নিয়মটো কাষত লিখি থওক। এটা সপ্তাহৰ ভিতৰতে পৃষ্ঠাখনে পত্ৰই ব্যৱহাৰ কৰিব পৰা প্ৰতিটো আৰ্হি ধৰি ৰাখে, আৰু পুনৰাবৃত্তি এখন পৃষ্ঠা পঢ়াৰ বিষয় হৈ পৰে। য'ত একেখিনি অলপ নিয়ম বাৰে বাৰে আহে, তেনে এটা বিষয়ত সেই পৃষ্ঠাখনে ৰখা নম্বৰৰ যোগ্য, আৰু ই ৰহস্যময় দেখা এটা বিষয়ক ধাঁধা প্ৰশ্ন আৰম্ভ হোৱাৰ আগতেই সাঁচি থোৱা এটা বিষয়লৈ ৰূপান্তৰ কৰে।

    শেষত, গতিৰ পৰা শুদ্ধতা বেছি গুৰুত্বপূৰ্ণ। খৰধৰকৈ দিয়া সংকেত প্ৰশ্ন এটাত সহজে সৰকণ ওলোটা কৰি বা সংযুক্ত নিয়মৰ ভুল অৰ্ধত প্ৰয়োগ কৰি উত্তৰ দিয়া হয়। যুক্তিতৰ্ক অংশই উদাৰ সময় দিয়ে, গতিকে পৰীক্ষাৰ্থীয়ে প্ৰতিটো যোৰ আখৰ লিখি গোটেই শব্দত নিয়ম পৰীক্ষা কৰিবলৈ সময় উলিয়াব পাৰে। এই নম্বৰবোৰ সাৱধানে সাঁচি, বিন্যাস আৰু ধাঁধা প্ৰশ্নক প্ৰয়োজনীয় সময় দিলে এজন ভালদৰে প্ৰস্তুত পৰীক্ষাৰ্থীয়ে ভাল যুক্তিতৰ্কৰ নম্বৰক উৎকৃষ্টলৈ ৰূপান্তৰ কৰে।

    Coding-Decoding for the RRB NTPC CBT

    Coding and decoding is one of the most rewarding topics in the Reasoning paper of the RRB NTPC examination. The paper sets two to four questions of this kind in CBT-1, and the same idea returns in CBT-2 and in the practice tests. A coding question gives a word and its code, then asks for the code of a new word or the word behind a new code. Because the rule is fixed once it is found, and because the same rule applies to every letter, these questions are among the fastest and safest marks on the page once the common patterns are familiar.

    Every coding question rests on a single idea: a rule turns one set of letters into another, and the same rule must be applied to the new word. The candidate is given one or two worked examples and must recover the rule from them. The good news is that the set of rules is small. Letter shifts, letter positions, reversal, opposites, and simple substitutions account for almost every question the paper sets. A candidate who learns these five families and practises each one a few times answers coding questions by recognition rather than by guesswork.

    The commonest family is the fixed letter shift. In this pattern each letter of the word is moved the same number of places along the alphabet. If CAT becomes DBU, each letter has moved one place forward, so PEN becomes QFO. If the shift is two places forward, PEN becomes RGP. The shift may be backward: if FISH becomes CFPE, each letter has moved three places back. The method is to compare the first letter of the word with the first letter of its code, find the size and direction of the shift, confirm it on the second letter, and then apply it to every letter of the new word.

    The second family replaces each letter by its alphabet position. In this pattern A becomes one, B becomes two, and so on up to Z which becomes twenty-six. If FACE is written as 6-1-3-5, then BEAD is written as 2-5-1-4. The same rule read backward decodes a number string: 8-5-12-12-15 is HELLO, because eight is H, five is E, twelve is L and fifteen is O. This family is the bridge between the alphabet series and coding, and it depends entirely on knowing the position of every letter instantly.

    The third family is reversal and rearrangement. If SMART is coded as TRAMS, the whole word has been written backward. If the first and last letters of COLD are swapped, the code is DOLC. If the middle two letters of a four-letter word are swapped, ROSE becomes RSOE. These rules act on the order of the letters rather than on their values, and they are recognised by the fact that the coded word contains exactly the same letters as the original, only in a different order. The moment a candidate sees that the letters match but the order has changed, the question is about arrangement and not about shifting.

    The fourth family is the opposite or mirror letter. Here each letter is replaced by the letter that stands the same distance from the other end of the alphabet: A pairs with Z, B with Y, C with X, and so on. Under this rule CAT becomes XZG, because C becomes X, A becomes Z and T becomes G. The pairings are fixed and worth memorising as a small table, because they appear often and they also underlie mirror-image questions. Once the pairs A-Z, B-Y, C-X, D-W and so on are known, this family is as quick as the fixed shift.

    The fifth family is simple substitution, where one word is mapped to another by a rule that is stated or shown. If in a code RAM is written as SBN, then each letter has moved one place forward and the rule is really the fixed shift in disguise. But some substitution questions use a rule that is specific to the example, such as each letter being replaced by the one two places ahead, or by the letter that is as far from Z as it is from A. The candidate must read the example carefully, state the rule in one sentence, and apply it exactly, without assuming a shift of one.

    Many questions combine two of these families, and this is where care is needed. A word may be reversed and then shifted, so MONEY becomes ZFOPN when it is written backward as YENOM and then each letter moved one place forward. A word may be shifted and then reversed. A code may move the first letter forward and the last letter backward, so CAT becomes DAS. When the rule is a combination, the candidate must identify the order of the steps, because reversing and shifting do not commute: doing them in the wrong order gives a different, wrong answer.

    The method for any coding question can be reduced to a short checklist. First, compare the given word with its code letter by letter. Second, decide whether the letters have the same identities in a different order (arrangement) or different identities in the same order (shifting or substitution). Third, for shifting, find the size and direction from the first letter and confirm on the second. Fourth, for position coding, convert the letters to numbers. Fifth, for opposites, use the mirror table. Sixth, if the rule combines two steps, fix the order and apply it to the whole word. Working through this checklist, on paper, is far faster than staring at the two words and guessing.

    The traps in coding and decoding are predictable. The commonest is applying the shift in the wrong direction, especially when the code runs backward toward Z. The second is a shift that changes partway through the word, which must be spotted by checking the rule on every letter and not only the first. The third is a combined rule applied in the wrong order. The fourth is a substitution question in which the candidate assumes a shift of one when the example actually shows a shift of two or three. The fifth is a position question in which the candidate miscounts a letter by one. Comparing the whole word with its code, letter by letter, and writing the pairs out, removes most of these errors.

    Coding and decoding is closely tied to several other reasoning topics. It is the alphabet series applied to a single word, so fluency in alphabet series makes it nearly effortless. The alphabet test and word formation use the same position table. Analogy asks for the relation between two items, which is the same skill of naming a rule and applying it. And mirror-image questions in the non-verbal section use the same opposite-letter pairs. A candidate who becomes comfortable here gains ground in three or four other topics at the same time.

    It is worth keeping a page in a notebook for the coding rules met in practice. Divide it into shift rules, position rules, arrangement rules and opposite rules, and write each new example under its heading with the rule beside it. Within a week the page holds every pattern the paper is likely to use, and revision becomes a matter of reading a single page. In a topic where the same handful of rules recurs again and again, that page is worth the marks it saves, and it turns a topic that looks mysterious into one that is banked before the puzzle questions even begin.

    Finally, accuracy matters more than speed. A coding question answered in a hurry is easily answered with the shift reversed or applied to the wrong half of a combined rule. The reasoning section gives generous time, so the candidate can afford to write each pair of letters and check the rule on the whole word. Banking these marks carefully, and leaving the arrangement and puzzle questions the time they need, is exactly how a well-prepared candidate turns a good reasoning score into a great one.

    Key facts to remember

    • Fixed shift: CAT → DBU (one place forward); confirm on the second letter.
    • Position code: FACE → 6-1-3-5, using A=1 to Z=26.
    • Reversal: SMART → TRAMS; swapping first and last: COLD → DOLC.
    • Opposite letters: A-Z, B-Y, C-X, so CAT → XZG.
    • Substitution: read the example, state the rule in one sentence, apply exactly.
    • Combined rules: reversing then shifting gives a different answer from shifting then reversing.
    • Decoding: run the rule backward, e.g. 8-5-12-12-15 is HELLO.
    • Traps: wrong direction, a shift that changes mid-word, wrong order in a combined rule.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the first step in any coding question?

    Compare the given word with its code letter by letter. Decide whether the letters have the same identities in a different order (arrangement) or different identities in the same order (shifting or substitution). That single decision tells you which family of rule is in play.

    How do I find the size of a letter shift?

    Take the first letter of the word and the first letter of its code, count the places between them in the alphabet, and note the direction. Confirm the same size and direction on the second letter before applying it to the whole new word.

    What are the opposite-letter pairs?

    A pairs with Z, B with Y, C with X, D with W, and so on; the two positions always add to twenty-seven. Under this rule CAT becomes XZG. Memorising the pairs makes both opposite-letter coding and mirror-image questions quick.

    How are combined rules such as reverse-then-shift handled?

    Identify the order from the example and apply the steps in that exact order, because the two steps do not commute. Reversing MONEY gives YENOM, and shifting each letter one place forward then gives ZFOPN; shifting first and reversing after would give a different result.

    Which other topics improve with coding-decoding practice?

    Alphabet series, the alphabet test, word formation, analogy and mirror-image questions. Coding is the alphabet shift applied to a whole word, so fluency in alphabet series makes coding nearly effortless.
